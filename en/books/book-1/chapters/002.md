Months passed.

In the center of the forest, where the stream flowed down toward the village, the sound of axes echoed through the morning.

Thump!

— You missed.

Thump!

— I didn't miss.

Zeph stopped his axe in mid-air and looked at Oskar.

— Do you want to chop in my place?

— No.

— Then shut up.

Oskar broke into a smile.

— I was right. The cut is crooked.

Zeph narrowed his eyes.

— It is not. Want to bet?

— Bet. If I bring mine down first, you carry my logs.

— And if I win, you clean everyone's fish today.

— Deal.

Both raised their axes.

— One... Two... Three!

The axes struck the wood in the same rhythm. Zeph adjusted his angle, Oskar kept close pace, while other loggers around them laughed at the pair and shook their heads.

Further down, on the shallow banks of the stream, the fishermen were pulling in their nets and sorting the catch into baskets.

— You're losing! — shouted Oskar, returning his attention to the contest.

— Keep dreaming! — Zeph shot back.

The crack widened. Zeph delivered one more precise blow.

CRACK!

— I think he won — said one of the older loggers.

The tree tilted and fell with a crash.

CRASH!

Leaves and branches kicked up a cloud of dust. Oskar opened his arms, but Zeph pointed at the trunk.

— It fell to my side. You lost. Go clean the fish.

Oskar placed a hand over his chest, feigning offense.

— Treason! Did anyone see this?

One of the fishermen, already coming up the bank with a heavy basket, shouted from afar:

— Zeph won! And if you're going to keep arguing, hurry up and help carry this over here!

The men laughed. That was how they worked. Axe, sweat, and timber, extracting their livelihood from Lord Gudran's Village. And the more the community grew, the deeper into the woods they had to go.

Zeph picked up his axe again.

— We need to chop the logs into smaller pieces and pack them into the wagons before it gets dark.

— Let's hurry! We have no time to lose.

Oskar grabbed his own, his tone growing a little more serious.

The loggers kept working.

Time was treacherous in the forest.

But everyone knew they had to respect the rule of the bell.

The birds fell silent.

Zeph looked at Oskar.

— It seems to be getting dark. Did you hear the bell?

— If the bell didn't ring, it's because it's not time to leave yet. I think you've been spending too much time with your father-in-law — Oskar replied, followed by laughter.

— I'm serious, Oskar.

Zeph raised his eyes to the sky.

The clouds were beginning to hide the day's last glow.

He sighed.

Oskar looked at him.

— In a rush?

Zeph offered a small smile.

— I'm a newly married man; I'm still not used to the idea that someone is waiting for me at home.

Oskar laughed.

— Liar. You're just afraid of arriving late and getting scolded.

— That too, Amara can be a bit fierce sometimes.

Ever since Amara had shared the news with him, returning home had gained a whole new meaning.

Now it wasn't just her waiting for him.

There was a little life growing inside her.

Zeph still couldn't believe he was going to be a father.

— I'm eager to get back home soon, my friend.

Oskar let out a chuckle.

— I bet. Amara must be waiting for you to arrive just to complain that the father-to-be has been working too hard.

Zeph smiled.

— She's already started with that.

— And did you think it would be any different? — Oskar shook his head.

— Now that you're going to be a father, my friend, you better get used to it. You're going to hear complaints in double measure.

Zeph laughed quietly.

— I just wanted to get home, sit at the table with Amara, and hear about her day.

Oskar patted his friend's shoulder.

— Have you thought of a name for the baby yet?

Zeph gave a soft laugh.

— Yes. If it's a girl, she'll be named Yara.

Oskar raised his eyebrows.

— Beautiful name. What does it mean?

— It means "lady of the waters."

— My mother used to say that names are like currents, which we choose to guide where the lives of those we love will flow. I want her to be free like a river no one can bind, strong enough to carve her own path through rock if she needs to.

Oskar fell silent for a moment.

— Aw... that was deep. I didn't know you had a brain under all that hair.

Zeph let out a laugh.

— And if it's a boy?

Zeph's smile faded slightly.

He looked at the axe in his hands before answering.

— Orion.

Oskar noticed the shift in his tone.

— Orion?

Zeph nodded.

— It was my father's name.

Oskar remained silent for a few seconds.

— You never talked much about him.

Zeph took a deep breath.

— I don't usually talk much about my father. My mother used to say he was a demon hunter and always tried to protect me, afraid that something might happen to me.

— A demon hunter?

— Yes. One of the best that ever lived — Zeph answered, sketching a small smile. — It's in my blood, believe it or not.

Zeph ran his thumb along the handle of the axe.

— She used to say he wasn't afraid of anything. That he faced creatures that would make any man run.

Oskar observed his friend in silence.

— Did you ever get to know him?

Zeph shook his head.

— No.

A brief pause.

— Fate didn't give me that chance.

Oskar lowered his eyes.

Zeph kept staring into the forest.

— I never knew what he was truly like. I only know the man my mother described.

He took a deep breath.

— Maybe naming my son after him is the only way I found to keep him alive... even if it's just in memory.

Oskar smiled discreetly.

— So you've decided everything then?

Zeph shook his head.

— Only the names.

Oskar laughed.

— And the rest?

Zeph looked at him.

— The rest... I think I'll learn when he arrives.

— It's good to see you like this. I never imagined that messy boy would turn into a serious man.

Zeph looked at him and smiled.

— Neither did I.

For a few seconds, both remained silent.

— Let's finish up quickly — Zeph said, grabbing the axe again.

— I didn't think you liked heavy work, Zeph.

— I like working, not testing my luck with dark approaching.

— Please, let's not get into that talk about dark stories again. — Oskar smiled.

— By the way, does your father-in-law still tell those stories about the forest?

Zeph let out a sigh.

— Darian only says what everyone in the village prefers to forget, Oskar. The forest has rules.

— Either way, we had better be out of here before nightfall.

Oskar nodded, his humor giving way to caution.

— Yeah. Respect the rule: it rings, you scram. But it hasn't rung yet.

Zeph offered a small smile.

— Don't get used to agreeing with me.

The two returned to work. Around them, the sound of laughter was gradually replaced by the steady rhythm of iron against wood.

But as the afternoon progressed, the environment began to change.

Dense, dark clouds rolled down from the mountain, quickly covering the sky. The golden light evaporated, replaced by an opaque, grey, and cold gloom.

Far away, near the river, the fishermen went silent.

— The bell... it should have rung by now — muttered Zeph, frowning and looking up.

— Maybe... maybe the bell got stuck.

— We'll only find out when we get back.

A gust of icy wind whipped through the trees and crawled along the ground.

Zeph looked down. A thin layer of white mist snaked silently between the roots, covering the earth and creeping up the men's boots.

Oskar took a step back, eyes wide with panic.

— Are you seeing this?

— I am — Zeph said, his heart racing. — Shift's over. Let me get back right now.

— Fully agreed! — stammered Oskar, already turning his body toward the group that was beginning to retreat in a hurry.

— Everyone get out! — yelled the senior logger up ahead, his voice echoing muffled by the rapidly thickening fog. — Gather what you can and move!

— Wait! I'm pulling in the nets — shouted a man near the riverbank.

— Head back now! Everyone to the wagons! — the old man yelled.

The men hurried and gathered everything they could.

— Go ahead, Oskar — Zeph said quickly. — I left my rope over there near the bank. I'll grab it in two seconds and catch up to you.

— Zeph, don't do anything foolish, look at this fog! — Oskar protested, pulling his friend's arm.

— Go now, I'm right behind you!

Without waiting, Zeph turned around and ran the few meters to the log.

He bent down, hurriedly untied the rope, and slung it over his shoulder.

— Done...

When he stood up, he heard something.

— Father...

Zeph froze.

The voice was faint.

So faint that, for a moment, he thought he had imagined it.

He looked around.

Nothing.

Just the mist.

— Who's there?

Silence.

Zeph narrowed his eyes, trying to see through the white.

Then he heard it again.

— Father, aren't you coming back?

This time, clearer.

It was the voice of a child.

Zeph felt a chill run down the back of his neck.

— Is someone there?

He took a step toward the voice.

Zeph stopped.

There was something about that voice.

Something familiar.

He couldn't explain what.

His heart tightened.

For a brief moment, he thought of Amara.

He thought of the child still being formed.

— Where are you?

Zeph moved forward.

One more step.

And another.

The voice seemed to always remain a few meters ahead.

— Father, why did you leave me?

— Wait! — he called out. — I'm right here!

No answer.

Zeph looked back.

The voices of Oskar and the other men had already disappeared.

He frowned.

The mist had closed in like a white wall, swallowing the trunks, the river, and the sound of the men. The world had vanished.

Zeph stopped, his breath caught in his throat.

— Oskar? — he called out, his voice tense.

No answer. Only the heavy silence of the forest surrounding him.

Then, cutting through the density of that absolute nothingness...

Dong.

The first toll of the bell sounded far off.

But it sounded too late.

And the sound seemed to come from everywhere at once.

Zeph remained motionless.

His hand was still gripping the rope.

His eyes tried to pierce through the fog, but there was nothing to see.

Nothing.

No trees.

No river.

Not even the ground a few steps ahead.

The mist was so thick it felt heavy. It wrapped around his legs, crept up his chest, and covered his face with a damp cold that made his skin crawl.

Zeph took a deep breath.

The air entered cold.

Shallow.

Heavy.

— Oskar?

His voice died just a few feet ahead.

No answer.

Zeph took a step.

His boot sank into the damp earth.

One more.

He reached out his hand.

His fingers touched something.

Wood.

A tree trunk.

Zeph released the breath he hadn't realized he was holding.

— Right...

He looked behind him.

Or tried to.

The fog had completely erased the path.

It was as if the world had been reduced to a small circle around his body.

Zeph turned slowly.

This time, he yelled.

— Oskar!

His heart began to beat faster.

He knew that forest.

He knew the trees.

He knew the river.

He knew the way back.

But now he recognized nothing.

The mist hid everything.

Zeph took a few more steps.

He stopped.

He waited.

Maybe he would hear something.

A shout.

Footsteps.

The creak of a wagon.

Anything.

Nothing.

Then...

Dong.

The second toll of the bell.

Zeph shuddered.

This time, the sound felt much closer.

So close that he had the impression the bell had chimed inside his own head.

Zeph tightened his grip on the rope.

— This isn't right.

His voice came out low.

He tried to find his way back by following his own footprints.

He crouched down.

The ground was too wet.

The boot marks had vanished.

Zeph ran his hand across the ground.

Nothing.

He stood up quickly.

Spun to one side.

Then to the other.

The mist remained motionless.

There was no wind.

There were no birds.

There were no insects.

There was no river.

Only that white.

That damned white.

Zeph began to walk faster.

— Oskar!

Nothing.

— Boys!

His breathing grew heavy. He accelerated: three steps, five, ten. He stopped and looked around.

The same place. Or at least it looked like the same place: the tree ahead, the partially buried stone, the thick root crossing the ground.

He had already passed by here. He was sure of it.

He turned back and walked in the opposite direction. Ten steps. Twenty. Thirty.

The tree appeared again.

Zeph froze. The blood seemed to drain from his face.

What is happening to my mind? This can't be real... Zeph thought.

He closed his eyes for a second, desperately seeking the memory of Amara's face, the warmth of her hands, her voice calling his name at the front door.

And then he remembered her words.

“You're going to be a father.”

Zeph opened his eyes.

He couldn't die here.

Not now.

For a moment, an old memory crossed his mind.

The absence.

The emptiness of growing up without knowing his own father's face. Without hearing his voice. Without having a hand to hold when fear came.

Zeph clenched his teeth.

I am not going to let this happen.

He brought his hand to his chest.

My son won't grow up like I did.

He won't wonder who his father was.

He won't grow up without knowing what it feels like to be loved by him.

Zeph took a deep breath, feeling his eyes sting.

— I will go back... — he whispered.

His voice came out trembling.

— I promise.

He couldn't die here.

Not when he finally had something beyond himself to protect.

He looked up.

He couldn't even see the branches.

The fog had swallowed everything.

Then he heard something.

A low sound.

Very distant.

Zeph raised his head.

Silence.

Then...

Thump.

Thump.

Thump.

It wasn't the bell.

It was wood.

As if something were striking against a tree.

Zeph remained still.

The sound came again.

Thump.

Pause.

Thump.

Pause.

Thump.

He gripped his axe.

— Who's there?

No answer.

Zeph took a step back.

The sound stopped.

And at that instant, he realized something that made his stomach turn to ice.

The fog behind him had moved.

Not like a cloud carried by the wind.

Like something that was breathing.

Zeph turned around slowly.

Nothing.

Just the white.

He tightened his grip on the handle of the axe.

— Come on...

He tried to convince himself.

— It's just fog.

— Just fog.

Another step.

— Nothing besides that.

But his own voice no longer sounded convinced.

On the other side of the mist, something moved.

Fast.

Zeph raised his axe.

— Who's there?!

Then a distant voice.

Faint.

It sounded like an old woman's.

Echoing through the fog.

— Zeph...

He froze.

Zeph surged forward immediately.

— Who are you? What do you want from me?

— Zeph...

The voice came again.

Further away.

Zeph ran.

The fog was closing in around him.

Every step seemed to take him into an even smaller space.

He couldn't breathe properly.

The air felt thick.

Warm amidst the cold.

His hand trembled on the handle of the axe.

The forest is consuming my mind! Damn it! Darian was always right about this cursed forest... Zeph thought.

Then he realized.

The voice hadn't come from ahead.

It came from behind.

Zeph stopped.

He turned slowly.

The fog was empty.

Completely empty.

And then the bell chimed for the third time.

DONG.

Zeph fell to his knees.

The sound struck through his body like a heavy blow.

For a few seconds, he couldn't think.

He couldn't move.

He only heard the echo fading away among the invisible trees.

Despair, cold and sharp as the fog, crashed down upon his chest. His clenched hands sank into the damp earth as he let out a stifled sob.

Amara... — he thought, his broken voice losing itself in the silence of the woods.

Please... forgive me. I just wanted to make it back in time. I wanted to see your smile again.

When he finally tried to stand up, he realized he was not alone.

Behind him, a hooded silhouette rose in silence.

Zeph's body turned cold as ice.

He turned around quickly.

Before he could comprehend what was standing before him, a bandaged hand emerged from the dense white and clamped around his neck. An inhuman force ripped him off the ground.

Zeph thrashed in panic. The axe dropped as his hands clawed at the creature's bandages, trying in vain to pry away that overwhelming strength.

For a few seconds, the creature merely stared at him in silence.

Zeph couldn't comprehend what he was seeing.

Then, through the fog behind the creature, something moved.

A figure emerged slowly, shuffling its feet.

A hunchbacked old woman, with cadaverous features and sunken eyes.

While the creature, in a abrupt movement, tightened its grip on Zeph's neck even further.

— Don't kill him. Not now... — the old woman hissed. — We need him alive.

— He is the key.
